ENGINEERING FAQ

Specify the conditions before treating glove or wet-touch as a feature.

These answers are for OEM and engineering review of custom projected-capacitive panels and capacitive touch keypads. They are not a released specification, test report, or performance guarantee. Items marked [PENDING CONFIRMATION] are still open. This page does not state glove-thickness bands, controller feature brand names, resistive part numbers, or laboratory metrics.

Can a PCAP touch panel work with gloves?

Often, for a defined OEM case, but glove behavior is configuration-specific. Share the glove material (for example nitrile, latex, leather, or a work glove), the approximate thickness if you know it, the expected gestures, and the cover construction. We review the sensor, cover thickness and coatings, and controller settings together, then agree the sample checks. Glove support is not a universal checkbox.

Which glove types and thicknesses should we specify?

List the gloves operators will actually wear, not a best-case laboratory glove. Note the material, whether the glove is lined, and the thickness if it is already known. A thin disposable medical glove and a thicker industrial work glove are different cases. When more than one glove applies, rank the scenarios that must be tuned and checked first.

Can users operate the panel with wet or damp hands?

Wet-hand behavior depends on how much moisture is present, how people gesture, the cover surface, and the controller water-handling settings. Light dampness and heavy wetting are different design cases. Describe the expected use, such as occasional damp hands or continuous wet work, so the checks represent that use. A general statement that wet-touch is acceptable is not a specification.

What is the difference between wet touch, dripping water, and condensation?

Damp or wet hands change the finger signal and the contact area. Droplets or flowing water can create false touches, or a temporary dead zone if water bridges sensing electrodes. Condensation or fogging can change surface conductivity over time. State which of these conditions apply to the equipment. Cover choice, controller settings, and mechanical sealing may need to be decided separately for each.

Does operation with water mean the product is waterproof or IP rated?

No. Touch response with water and protection against water ingress are separate requirements. A panel can be tuned for wet-touch use without meeting a stated IP rating. An IP-rated enclosure does not by itself produce clean wet-touch behavior. State both needs independently in the inquiry. Neither is a blanket guarantee.

Why do water droplets cause false touches on capacitive screens?

Water changes local capacitance. A droplet can be reported as a touch, or it can spread the signal across electrodes. Some controllers include settings intended to reduce water-related false touches. Those settings often trade off against sensitivity, including sensitivity through gloves. Agree what balance of false touches and missed touches is acceptable for the interface, then check samples under the droplet conditions you defined.

Can we use multi-touch, including pinch and zoom, while wearing gloves?

Multi-touch with gloves is more demanding than a single tap or swipe. Thick gloves and moisture both reduce signal margin. If multi-touch is required, list it as a must-have early. Sensor and controller selection, and the sample tests, should cover multi-finger gestures with the specified gloves, not only single taps.

How do medical, industrial, and HVAC cases differ?

Medical interfaces often use thinner disposable gloves and frequent cleaning chemicals. False activation and cleanability matter. Industrial HMIs more often involve thicker work gloves, electrical noise from machinery, and harsher environments. HVAC and water-heating controls often emphasize droplets, wet hands, and unintended activation. Bring that application context so the requirements stay realistic.

What do you need from us to evaluate glove or wet-touch performance?

At minimum: glove types (photos help), moisture scenarios, cover material and thickness targets, the host interface, approximate size, and the critical actions (tap, swipe, or multi-touch). Drawings help, but they are not required to start.

How do controller tuning and sample validation work?

After the stack — sensor, cover, and mechanics — is defined, controller parameters are adjusted for the agreed glove and moisture cases, then checked on engineering samples under those conditions. Expect iteration. Sensitivity, noise immunity, and water handling often pull in opposite directions. Production intent should match the validated sample stack. A later change in cover thickness or glove type needs another check.

When might resistive touch still be discussed?

Resistive touch can remain relevant when thick gloves, a stylus, or certain sealed environments dominate, and when multi-touch or optical clarity are secondary. When glove and wet conditions can be defined and checked, many OEM teams still prefer PCAP for optics, outer-surface durability, and interface flexibility. The comparison should follow the real operator conditions.

Will cleaning chemicals or wet wiping change glove or wet-touch tuning?

Cleaning agents affect coatings and the long-term surface condition. A wet wipe can momentarily look like water exposure to the sensor. Share the specified cleaners and how often the surface is wiped, especially for medical and food-adjacent equipment. Material compatibility and momentary wet-touch behavior should both be reviewed.

Can a capacitive touch keypad handle moisture better than a full PCAP panel?

A fixed-function capacitive keypad can use button geometry, spacing, and firmware aimed at wet or gloved use. It is not automatically immune to water bridging or thick gloves. Key count, icon layout, and the expected wet or glove conditions still need to be defined. Compare a keypad with a full PCAP panel against the interface and the environment. Neither is a universally safer choice.

What should the inquiry say so glove and wet-touch claims stay precise?

Use measurable scenarios the customer can fill in. Examples of the form of a statement, not published DaMing limits, are: nitrile gloves with the thickness stated by the customer, single tap and swipe; occasional droplets on the cover during a rinse; continuous running water is not assumed unless that case is validated. Avoid absolute language such as “works with any glove” or “100% waterproof touch.” Drawings, sample plans, and later public claims should keep the same precision.
